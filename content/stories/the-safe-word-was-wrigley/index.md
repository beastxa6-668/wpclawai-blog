Sam and I have done long distance for fourteen months. Chicago to Denver is a two-and-a-half-hour flight, a one-hour time difference, and roughly four thousand FaceTime calls where one of us falls asleep first.

It's usually me. I teach eighth-grade science in Logan Square. By nine p.m. I've been explaining density to thirteen-year-olds for so long that I'm basically a rock myself.

The call where it came up was a Sunday. Sam was on his balcony in Denver with a beer, and I was on my couch with a bowl of cereal for dinner, which he'd stopped commenting on.

"Can I ask you something weird," he said.

"You're asking permission to be weird? That's new."

He laughed, then didn't say anything for long enough that I put the cereal down.

"You know how you said once, about the—" He made a vague gesture with his free hand. "The tie thing. In that show."

I did know. I'd said it nine months ago, half joking, after a TV episode, and I'd assumed he'd forgotten. Sam does not forget things. He remembers my students' names.

*Oh no. He remembered. Now I have to be a person who meant it.*

"I was kidding," I said, which was not totally true.

"Okay," he said. Just okay. No push. That's the thing about Sam. He'll ask once, and then he'll wait, and the waiting is somehow worse than pushing.

I picked the cereal back up and ate three bites while he watched me.

"I wasn't totally kidding," I said.

---

We spent the next two weeks talking about it in the least sexy way possible, which turned out to be the right way.

We made a Google Doc. I'm not joking. It was titled *Thanksgiving Logistics*, because Sam was flying in for the holiday and we had a shared doc with his flight info anyway. Under the flight number and my mom's address for dinner, there was a section that said things like *yes*, *maybe*, *no*, and *ask me first*.

Sam put *no* next to a few things I had expected to be on his *yes* list. I put *maybe* next to things I had expected to be *no*. We both put *ask me first* next to almost everything else.

We picked a safe word on a call while I was grading quizzes. He suggested "pineapple." I said that was what everybody used. He said, "Fine, Professor, you pick." I said "Wrigley," because the Cubs had broken my heart enough times that the word already meant *stop*.

I read the whole description twice in the teachers' lounge with my screen dimmed all the way down.

What came in it: a collar with a chain leash, two wrist cuffs, two ankle cuffs, a ball gag, a blindfold, a flogger, and a hogtie connector. Snakeskin-textured faux leather with soft fleece lining. Adjustable buckle straps. The part I read three times was at the bottom, under care and safety: never leave the wearer unattended, keep checking comfort and breathing and circulation, use only with clear consent and safe words, don't over-tighten.

I don't know why, but seeing that written down by the people *selling* it made me trust the whole thing more.

It came in plain outer packaging, nothing on the label, two days before Sam's flight. My downstairs neighbor brought it up because it had been left at her door. She handed it to me and said, "Your mom sending you snacks again?" and I said, "Something like that."

---

When Sam got to my apartment Wednesday night, he was cold and starving and smelled like the airplane. We ate leftover pad see ew on the couch. The box sat on my bookshelf between a Dutch oven and a stack of ungraded lab reports, and neither of us looked at it for an hour.

Then he did.

"Is that—"

"Yep."

"Can I see?"

I opened it on the coffee table like we were defusing something. Sam picked up one of the wrist cuffs, turned it over and said, "It looks like a purse."

"It's snakeskin *texture*."

"It looks like a purse my aunt would carry to a casino."

I laughed so hard I knocked over the pad see ew.

We put the ball gag back in the box first. Neither of us had marked that one *yes*. The flogger and the hogtie connector went back too. Not a no, exactly. Just a not-this-time. That left the blindfold and the wrist cuffs. That was the list.

---

It took forever to get the buckles right. I'm serious. The first try was too loose and his wrist slid out. The second was too tight and I panicked and undid it so fast I scratched him with my thumbnail. He said "ow" and then "I'm fine, I'm fine," and I made him show me his wrist anyway, under the lamp, like I was checking a kid on the playground.

Somewhere around the third try, I stopped feeling like a teacher.

I'm not going to describe all of it. I'll just say it was quieter than I expected. The blindfold made him go very still, and the stillness made me slow down. I kept asking "okay?" and he kept saying "okay," and at some point his "okay" sounded different, lower, and my hands weren't shaking anymore. His breathing changed. Mine did too. The room got warm. At one point we were both laughing, really laughing, and then neither of us was.

Nobody said *Wrigley*. But I knew it was there. That was the whole point.

---

Afterward, I undid the cuffs, checked his wrists again under the lamp, and he let me.

"You're doing the teacher thing," he said.

"Hold still."

On Thanksgiving my mom asked us how the visit was going so far. Sam said, "Really good," with a completely straight face, and passed the green beans.

The box is back on my shelf now, next to the Dutch oven. Sam flies home Sunday. We already updated the doc.
