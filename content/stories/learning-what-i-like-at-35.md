At 7:40 on a Wednesday morning, I was sitting on the edge of my bed in Clintonville, holding coffee I'd microwaved twice. My navy scrubs smelled like hand sanitizer. There was a sock on the floor that wasn't going to pick itself up. I was thinking about something a patient had said six hours earlier.

“Honey, what do you like?”

She'd been waiting for a CT and we'd been talking about dinner. That was all. A perfectly normal question about food, asked by an eighty-one-year-old woman who had very firm opinions about hospital toast.

I had answered something about Thai takeout. Then I'd spent the rest of my shift realizing how often I said, “Whatever you want.”

I was thirty-four. Almost thirty-five, which sounds close enough until someone puts it on a birthday card. Kyle had moved out last spring with the good skillet and the Roku. We'd been together six years, and apparently I had spent a fair amount of that time agreeing with him.

Not because he demanded it. That would've been easier to explain.

*What if I stopped agreeing and there wasn't much else underneath?*

I put the coffee down. It was cold again.

---

Two nights later, Dani caught me looking at the vending machine like it had personally disappointed me.

“Pretzels,” she said.

“I wasn't asking.”

“You've been standing there for a full minute.”

Dani had been an ER nurse for eleven years. She could tell when somebody needed a chair, water, or to stop pretending they were fine. With coworkers, she was slightly less diplomatic.

In the break room I told her about the toast lady. About the question. Not the whole six-year history, but enough.

“So what do you like?” she asked.

“Excellent. A follow-up.”

She waited.

I said I honestly wasn't sure. Not just food. Other things. Things I didn't particularly want to discuss next to a microwave with a laminated cleaning schedule.

Dani didn't give me a speech. She asked whether I ever let myself try something without deciding beforehand whether I was any good at it.

“I don't think that's how trying works,” I said.

“Interesting that you know that.”

---

The next morning, I ordered [a compact cat-shaped air-pulse toy](https://sindept.com/products/cat-shaped-clitoral-suction-toy-7-modes/?utm_source=velvetnotes&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=learning-what-i-like-at-35&utm_content=inline) with seven patterns. The description said silicone, rechargeable, IPX7, and a quiet motor. I read that last part more than once. My upstairs neighbor coughed and I heard it in my kitchen. That line helped me put the phone down and leave the order alone.

The packaging line mattered, too: plain outer packaging, no product details on the label. When the box arrived and Mr. Okafor from 1B asked if I'd gotten something good, I said, “Printer ink.”

He nodded. Nobody asks a follow-up question about printer ink.

I left it on my nightstand for a while. That sounds ridiculous, but I liked knowing it was there before I felt ready to do anything about it. I had work. I had laundry. I had a very good ability to make ordinary errands last until bedtime.

On Sunday, I was off until Tuesday. I showered, put on my old Kenyon T-shirt, and lit a candle called Autumn Hearth, despite it being warm enough that I'd opened the window.

I lay across the middle of the bed.

I didn't have a side anymore. I hadn't really noticed that.

---

Nothing happened on schedule. That was the first thing.

I kept thinking about work. Then about whether I was doing this wrong. Then about the fact that even alone, in my own apartment, I had somehow turned a quiet evening into an assignment.

After a few minutes I almost stopped. Netflix was right there. So was a basket of towels, if I really wanted to commit to avoiding myself.

Instead I took a breath and let my shoulders drop. I didn't have to make the evening impressive. Nobody was waiting for a report.

Some things felt good. Some didn't. I changed my mind. Once, something startled a laugh out of me, and the laugh helped more than I would've expected. I could hear a car on the street and the low sound of somebody's television through the wall. My candle smelled aggressively like October.

For a little while, I wasn't evaluating anything. I was just warm, and there, and not trying to say the right thing.

Afterward, I lay still with my hand over my mouth. I laughed again. Then I cried a little, which was annoying, because I hadn't brought tissues and the box was on the dresser.

I got up and got them. Nothing cinematic about it.

---

I wish I could say I woke up as a woman who knew exactly what she wanted. On Monday I stood in the Kroger cereal aisle for eight minutes and bought the same thing as always.

But I noticed myself doing it.

At the Thai place later that week, I nearly ordered the pad see ew because Kyle always liked it. Then I remembered that Kyle wasn't there and, frankly, neither was his opinion about noodles.

I got the drunken noodles. Extra spicy.

When the waiter asked how everything was, I took another bite before I answered.

“Really good,” I said.

It was. Not a declaration of independence. Dinner.

The next morning Dani looked up from her charting.

“You're humming.”

“I'm not.”

“You are.” She narrowed her eyes. “Don't tell me.”

“I wasn't going to.”

“Good. We work together.”

She went back to the screen, smiling.

I still say “whatever” sometimes. I still overthink easy decisions.

But when somebody asks what I like, I try not to answer before I've checked.
