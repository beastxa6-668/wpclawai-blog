The cart timer wasn't real. Theo knew that. There was no timer on the screen, no clock ticking down above the checkout button. Still, at 9:43 on a Tuesday, he felt as if he'd been standing at a cash register long enough to annoy the people behind him.

He was alone in his kitchen. Biscuit was asleep against the refrigerator.

The refrigerator made a hard clicking noise. Theo flinched and closed the laptop halfway, which was an interesting response to an appliance that had never expressed an opinion about him.

“Good,” he told the dog. “Very normal.”

Biscuit's ear moved. That was the extent of the feedback.

He opened the laptop again.

He'd spent the evening doing the thing he did when a decision embarrassed him: collecting enough information to disguise it as work. A notebook lay beside the computer. He had made columns. He'd crossed out one heading and replaced it with **cleaning**, because apparently he could face this subject if it resembled a maintenance schedule.

On the screen was [the automatic stroker he'd been comparing](https://sindept.com/products/automatic-male-masturbator-7-vibration-3-suction-modes/). He had read about the detachable sleeve and the controls. He had looked at the price. Then he'd looked at the price again, as though a different number might settle the question he was avoiding.

Thirty-two dollars and ninety-nine cents was not the problem. His last emergency hardware-store trip had cost more than that, and all he'd come home with was a fitting that didn't fit.

*What kind of man needs to buy this?*

There it was. Nothing to do with the price.

Theo was forty-one, divorced for three years, living in Tucson with a dog who considered a closed bathroom door a serious breach of household policy. He could present a storm-drainage plan to a room full of people determined to find something wrong with it. He could ask a contractor to redo a measurement without apologizing for asking.

He could not apparently put a private purchase in a shopping cart without conducting a character investigation.

His phone lit up. The college group chat had produced fourteen messages about somebody's grill.

Kev had sent a photograph of a steak. Danny had replied that it looked like a roofing sample.

Theo smiled. He typed **Pretty sure that needs flashing** and sent it.

For a second he considered asking a question. Not the whole question. Just something vague enough to pass as a joke.

He put the phone facedown instead.

The laptop had dimmed. His reflection appeared faintly over the page: old T-shirt, glasses sliding down, the expression he wore when a parking meter refused to accept his card.

He'd imagined feeling caught. By whom, exactly, remained unclear. His ex-wife still had a key for the days she helped with Biscuit, but she wasn't coming over tonight. No one was waiting outside to inspect the browser history. The dog was now licking one foot with the concentration of a specialist.

Theo adjusted his glasses and read the care instructions once more.

That, at least, was a real question. He could wash something. He could let it dry. He could find a place to keep it that wasn't underneath the sink with a leaking bottle of furniture polish.

He got up and opened the hallway cupboard.

A flashlight rolled into his shoe. Behind it were two dead batteries, a leash Biscuit had chewed as a puppy, and the charging cable for a phone he'd stopped owning several years ago.

“Excellent system,” Theo said.

He moved the batteries into a small dish. He coiled the cable. Then he stood there holding the flashlight, aware that he'd started organizing a cupboard rather than making a decision.

Biscuit appeared behind him. Cupboards sometimes contained treats. The dog was willing to investigate.

“Not everything is for you.”

Biscuit sat down anyway.

Theo put the flashlight back, got the current leash from its hook and took him outside.

The pavement still held some of the day's heat. At the corner, a sprinkler was watering a narrow strip of driveway with complete confidence. Biscuit stopped to smell the same utility pole he'd smelled that morning.

For twenty minutes, Theo wasn't a man deciding what a purchase meant. He was a man waiting for a dog to finish reading a pole.

When they got home, he filled the water bowl and washed his hands. The laptop was where he'd left it. The cart was still there.

He sat down. He moved the pointer over checkout, then away.

He didn't want to buy something simply to prove he wasn't embarrassed. That seemed like letting the embarrassment make the decision from the other direction.

He clicked remove.

There was no dramatic sense of relief. He still felt curious. He still felt awkward. He was also tired, and he had an early meeting about a culvert that nobody had photographed properly.

In the notebook, under the columns, he wrote: **Can look again.**

He nearly added an explanation. He didn't.

Then he saved the page, shut the laptop and went to find the dog, who had already taken the warm side of the bed.
