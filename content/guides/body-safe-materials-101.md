Maya had three tabs open and a bowl of cereal going soft beside her laptop. One product said silicone. Another said TPE. A third said “premium,” which wasn't helping her compare anything. She was thirty-four, newly single, and perfectly capable of choosing her own cereal. Apparently choosing something for herself required a vocabulary test.

She clicked back to the details, looking for a material list instead of another promise. Did “soft” mean silicone? Could she wash the whole thing? If it had a charging port, did waterproof mean the same thing as easy to clean? She closed the tab that answered none of those questions. Two left. That felt like progress.

## Quick answer

> Start with a clearly stated material and cleaning instructions for the exact item. 100% silicone and silicone blends are not interchangeable labels. TPE may be porous and needs particular care. “Body-safe,” “premium” and “soft” are not substitutes for product-specific evidence. If you cannot confirm the material or suitable care, pause rather than guess.

## What does “body-safe” actually tell me?

Start with the contact surface: its material, any coatings or mixed materials, and the manufacturer's cleaning instructions. Look for the exact composition—100% silicone or a silicone blend—and check the care guidance alongside it.

If you have a known sensitivity, a history of irritation, or symptoms you're concerned about, get individual advice from a qualified clinician.

## Silicone versus TPE: what is the useful distinction?

[Planned Parenthood's material guidance](https://www.plannedparenthood.org/learn/sex-pleasure-and-sexual-dysfunction/sex-and-pleasure/sex-toys) distinguishes nonporous 100% silicone from potentially porous materials such as TPE, TPR and silicone blends. Porosity matters because washing a surface isn't the same as reliably removing everything absorbed within it.

| Label you see | What to check before buying | What not to assume |
|---|---|---|
| Silicone | Exact composition, contact surfaces, cleaning and lubricant instructions | That silicone blends have the same properties as 100% silicone |
| TPE / TPR | Whether the material is porous and the maker's care and barrier guidance | That a soft, realistic feel means it cleans like nonporous silicone |
| ABS plastic | Which parts are ABS, joins between materials, and cleaning instructions | That a rigid housing tells you the material of a separate contact surface |
| “Premium,” “soft” or “body-safe” | An actual material specification and evidence behind the claim | That these words identify a material or a testing standard |

You don't need to memorize a catalog of polymers. You do need an answer more specific than “luxury feel.” If the description is unclear, ask before ordering. A retailer who cannot identify the contact material has not given you enough to make an informed choice.

## Is TPE toxic? Is TPE safe for a sex toy?

Short answer: "TPE" alone doesn't tell you either way. TPE (thermoplastic elastomer, sometimes sold as TPR) is a family of soft, rubbery plastics, not one recipe, so two toys labeled TPE can be made quite differently. What the evidence does support:

- **It's treated as porous.** Sexual-health guidance groups TPE and TPR with materials that can hold onto bacteria in a way nonporous 100% silicone doesn't. In a small 2014 study published in *Sexually Transmitted Infections*, HPV was still found on a TPE vibrator in 5 of 9 cases right after cleaning, and in 2 of 5 of those a day later; on the silicone vibrator it was found in 4 of 9 right after cleaning and in none after 24 hours ([PubMed 24739872](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24739872/)). Twelve participants is a small sample, but it's a useful reason not to share TPE toys without a condom.
- **"Jelly" is a different and bigger worry.** A Danish Environmental Protection Agency survey found that toys sold as "jelly" were plasticized PVC with plasticizer content up to 70%, mostly phthalates such as DEHP ([Danish EPA, 2006](https://www2.mst.dk/udgiv/publications/2006/87-7052-227-8/html/helepubl_eng.htm)). It also noted oil-based lubricants increase how much plasticizer migrates. If a listing says "jelly" without naming the material, treat that as missing information.
- **"Phthalate-free" is a claim, not a test result.** Unless the seller can point to testing for that product, read it as the maker's statement.

### What we'd do with a TPE toy

1. Prefer 100% silicone for anything used internally or shared.
2. If you do choose TPE (common for strokers and realistic sleeves because of the feel), keep it to one person, or use a condom over it.
3. Use water-based lube, wash with warm water and mild soap after every use, and let it dry completely before storing it on its own.
4. Replace it if it turns sticky, cloudy, cracked or starts to smell.

**Searchers also ask:** *What is TPE silicone?* There isn't such a thing; TPE and silicone are separate materials, and "TPE silicone" usually means a listing that's mixing up the two. *TPR vs silicone?* TPR is a type of TPE, so the same porous-versus-nonporous difference applies.

## Can I boil it, soak it or put it in the dishwasher?

Not unless the instructions for that exact product say you can. A material's general properties don't tell you what a motor, adhesive, seam or charging connection can tolerate.

A water-resistance rating is also not a sterilization instruction. Don't turn “water-resistant” into “safe to leave underwater,” and don't transfer one item's IPX7 rating to another product in the same collection.

[NHS sexual-health guidance](https://www.sexualhealthsthelens.nhs.uk/adult---pleasure) recommends cleaning before and after use, with attention to joins, buttons and battery compartments. Follow the specific care instructions, let the item dry fully, and store it separately as directed. If the surface becomes damaged, sticky, cracked or difficult to clean, stop using it and check with the manufacturer rather than trying to cover the problem with a stronger cleaner.

## Which lubricant goes with silicone?

Water-based lubricant is a straightforward starting point when the product instructions allow it. [Oxford University Hospitals' sexual-health service](https://www.sexualhealthoxfordshire.nhs.uk/services/psychosexual-therapy/lubricants/) advises that water-based lubricants are compatible with sex toys, while silicone and some oil-based lubricants can degrade silicone or realistic-feel materials over time.

Read both labels. Compatibility with a toy and compatibility with a condom are separate questions; oil-based lubricants can damage condoms. Don't assume that a bottle described as natural is automatically suitable for either.

## What should make me pause?

A missing material list. Cleaning instructions that contradict the water-resistance claim. A seller using “medical-grade” or “certified” without identifying a verifiable standard or test. A product that arrives damaged or differs from its description.

Those aren't invitations to improvise. Keep the item unused while you ask questions or check return eligibility. For intimate items, opening or using them can change what you can return; read the current policy first.

## FAQ

### Is every silicone toy nonporous?

Don't assume so from the word alone. Planned Parenthood specifically distinguishes 100% silicone from silicone blends. If composition isn't confirmed, ask rather than upgrading the claim yourself.

### Is TPE automatically the same as silicone?

No. They're different material labels. Treat TPE as potentially porous and check the maker's cleaning and barrier guidance. A similar texture doesn't establish similar properties.

### Does a waterproof rating mean I can boil a toy?

No. Waterproofing and heat tolerance are different. Boiling can damage electronics and other components. Use only methods expressly allowed for the exact item.

### How should I care for a toy when sharing?

Clean before and after use, and use a new condom when sharing between people or switching body openings. Seek medical advice about pain, irritation or other symptoms.
